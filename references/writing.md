# Writing application materials

## Language and positioning

Use the language requested by the user. If none is specified, normally match the vacancy language; preserve established materials in their existing language when that is clearly intentional.

Choose the positioning from the vacancy and verified evidence:

- hands-on roles: emphasize delivery, troubleshooting, operations, and relevant implementation depth;
- leadership roles: emphasize verified scope, decisions, mentoring, planning, and organizational outcomes;
- reliability roles: emphasize verified availability, incident response, observability, recovery, and prevention practices;
- platform roles: emphasize verified reusable systems, developer enablement, delivery workflows, and operating standards;
- regulated or isolated environments: emphasize verified security, audit, offline, compliance, and deployment experience.

These are emphasis choices, not permission to add missing experience.

## CV or resume

Prefer a clear, conventional structure unless the user supplies a template:

1. name, target role, and selected contact details;
2. concise summary;
3. professional experience in reverse chronological order;
4. technical or professional skills;
5. education, certifications, languages, or other relevant sections.

Writing rules:

- Lead bullets with the outcome or ownership, then explain the action and relevant tools.
- Use metrics exactly as sourced and only in the role or employer context supported by the evidence.
- Keep each bullet focused on one achievement or responsibility.
- Match important vacancy terminology only when it truthfully describes verified experience.
- List only verified skills. Reorder a verified skill to match the vacancy; never add a skill for keyword coverage alone.
- Preserve chronology. If achievements cannot be assigned to individual roles within one employer, use a company-level achievements block and list role progression separately.
- Do not claim knowledge of a tool because the candidate used a similar tool. Describe the transferable adjacent experience instead.
- Avoid internal jargon that a recruiter cannot interpret.

Honor a requested page limit. If none is specified, prefer one page when the relevant evidence fits legibly and use two pages when seniority or relevant history warrants it. Do not sacrifice truth, meaningful evidence, or readability solely to force one page.

## Summary

Write a short paragraph, normally 50–90 words, covering the target role, relevant years or scope when verified, core evidence, and one or two high-value outcomes. Avoid keyword lists, unsupported nice-to-haves, and first-person prose unless the user's preferred style uses it.

## Cover letter or application note

Create one only when requested or required by the application. Keep it concise, normally 3–6 sentences and 50–120 words unless the form sets another limit.

- Open with one specific, sourced reason for interest in the company, product, mission, scale, or problem.
- Connect one verified candidate achievement to the role's central challenge.
- Mention a material gap or practical constraint only when it helps set honest expectations.
- Avoid generic praise, exaggerated enthusiasm, and a summary of the entire CV.
- Do not invent a company fact. If no reliable fact is available, write a direct role-focused opening instead.
- Match the destination's tone and use the candidate's preferred sign-off.

## Screening answers

Answer from verified facts and follow the requested format or character limit. For yes/no questions, do not convert adjacent experience into a false `yes`. Explain briefly when the form permits; otherwise stop and ask the user when the distinction is material.

## Final factual review

Check:

- every name, contact field, employer, title, date, technology, certification, and metric;
- role attribution and tense;
- spelling of the company and target role;
- absence of template placeholders and private data the user did not request;
- consistency among the CV, cover letter, and screening answers.
